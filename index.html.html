Section 1

The carbon pricing gap

Colombia's carbon tax is supposed to make polluters pay for the harm they cause. In practice, most emitters receive a subsidy — not a penalty. Here's what that looks like compared to what the science says.

Only 13% of Colombia's greenhouse gas emissions faced any positive carbon price in 2023. Road transport — which moves most of the country's food — had less than 3% of emissions priced, with the rest effectively subsidised at EUR 77 per tonne.

What would reform achieve? Raising the carbon tax to EUR 60 per tonne — still far below the scientific estimates — would cut emissions by 40% against Colombia's current climate target by 2030, and generate up to 1% of national income in additional government revenue annually. That's roughly equivalent to the cost of diesel subsidies in 2023.
Section 2

Why it matters more for Colombia

The global carbon price benchmark assumes a global average of damages. Colombia is not average. It is one of the world's most biodiverse countries, already experiencing accelerating ecosystem losses — and it contributes less than 0.5% of global emissions.

Páramos: Colombia's water towers

High-altitude wetlands called páramos supply approximately 70% of Colombia's freshwater — to cities, farms, and hydropower plants. They are also among the most threatened ecosystems on Earth.

Water supply and ecosystem threat data.

Ecosystem health is declining

Colombia's Ocean Health Index fell from 67 to 65 between 2014 and 2024 — below the global average of 69. Over half the national territory has already lost ecological integrity. More than two-thirds of ecosystems are now classified as threatened.

OHI trend data.

Armed conflict drives deforestation

Armed groups and illicit coca cultivation have driven the loss of more than 3 million hectares of forest over two decades, releasing 1.3 billion tonnes of CO₂. In 2023, 79 land defenders were killed in Colombia — 40% of all reported cases globally.

Deforestation trend data.

A double fiscal exposure

Colombia earns about 1.8% of GDP annually from fossil fuel taxes and royalties. At the same time, a full fossil fuel phase-out would cost up to 3% of GDP and 100,000 jobs. It is both a victim of climate damages and a potential loser from global decarbonisation.

At 2%: This is the rate used by recent scientific consensus (Burke et al., 2026 in Nature). It implies that the welfare of future generations matters nearly as much as our own. The result is USD 1,013 per tonne — 124 times Colombia's current carbon price.
Why Colombia's case is different: Standard models use a global average discount rate. But Colombia bears 2–10 times the global average climate damage per tonne of emissions — and its most critical ecosystems (páramos, the Amazon) face irreversible thresholds. Once crossed, no future investment can undo the loss. For those assets, the appropriate discount rate approaches zero.
Section 4

Tipping points and irreversible losses

A tipping point is a threshold beyond which a change becomes self-reinforcing and essentially permanent. Standard carbon price models treat all damages as gradual. They do not account for what happens when an ecosystem crosses a point of no return.

The Amazon tipping point is estimated at roughly 20–25% total deforestation. Beyond this, reduced rainfall and increased fire risk trigger further forest loss without any additional human action. Colombia's 2024 deforestation resurgence (+35%) is pushing the country closer to this threshold.

The option that disappears: Colombia's scientific analysis identifies silvopastoral systems (integrated cattle and tree farming in Amazonian grasslands) as the single largest domestic climate solution — equivalent to 208 million tonnes of CO₂ at negative net cost. This option only exists while those grasslands remain ecologically intact. Once the tipping point is crossed, it is permanently forfeited.

The correlated risk problem

Standard models treat physical climate risk and fiscal transition risk as independent. For Colombia, they are correlated — the same scenarios that produce the worst ecological damage also produce the worst fiscal outcomes.

Worst physical scenario

Fast warming → ecosystem collapse → higher spending on disaster response, water infrastructure, agricultural support

Worst fiscal scenario

Fast global decarbonisation → Colombia's fossil fuel revenues collapse → less money for adaptation precisely when it's most needed

Both scenarios tend to happen together. The carbon price formula has no way to model this interaction.

What the carbon price cannot see

The models used to calculate the carbon price are built around GDP — the value of goods and services that pass through markets. Nature's contribution to the economy does not pass through markets. So it does not count.

The Gross Ecosystem Product (GEP): measuring what GDP misses

Researchers have developed a way to put a monetary value on nature's services using the same accounting logic as GDP. A pilot study in Colombia's Sinú Basin found that ecosystem services were worth 1.7% of regional GDP annually — value completely invisible to standard economic analysis.

The chart below shows what happened in Qinghai Province, China — a high-altitude water tower for downstream populations, structurally similar to Colombia's páramos and Amazonia. The economy grew dramatically (GDP ×8 between 2000 and 2015), but the value nature provides remained enormous throughout. In 2000, GEP actually exceeded GDP. By 2015, despite massive economic growth, GEP was still three-quarters the size of GDP.

The key point: economic growth did not replace or reduce what nature was worth — it just made nature's contribution less visible relative to the growing market economy. In Colombia, the same pattern applies. The páramos, forests and wetlands are generating enormous economic value that never appears in national statistics or carbon price calculations.

Qinghai Province, China — GDP vs nature's contribution (GEP), year 2000 and year 2015

What this means for Colombia: The water páramos supply to Bogotá, the erosion forests prevent around hydropower reservoirs, the floods wetlands absorb in the Magdalena Valley — all of this has real economic value. None of it appears in the carbon price calculation. A country that manages these assets well is generating wealth that the standard economic framework cannot see.
Section 6

Four actions that are achievable now

All four recommendations are grounded in existing laws, existing institutions, and existing data. None requires new legislation. Each addresses a specific failure the carbon price framework cannot fix on its own.

✓

Complete diesel subsidy reform In progress

Petrol subsidies were eliminated in 2023 — a significant fiscal achievement. Diesel subsidies remain, costing 1.3% of GDP in 2023. Linking diesel prices to international market levels would generate 0.5–0.7% of GDP in fiscal savings. A structured three-step plan exists: restart stakeholder dialogues with transport operators; protect vulnerable households through Colombia's existing social welfare registry (Sisbén); develop medium-term freight sector modernisation support. The distributional risks are real — Andean geography forces near-total road dependence, and Pacific coastal fishers are particularly exposed — but the data show that current subsidies are regressive: according to OECD (2026), the top four income deciles capture the largest share of subsidy benefits. The poorest households benefit least from the subsidy but would be most affected by food price increases if reform is badly sequenced.

⟳

Accelerate the Emissions Trading System Delayed

Colombia's 2021 Climate Action Law mandated an Emissions Trading System (ETS) covering 45% of national emissions from industry and energy. As of mid-2025, it has not started. The country's highest inter-ministerial climate body (CICC) can issue a formal binding calendar for the required monitoring and verification steps. Until these sectors face a real carbon price, carbon pricing reform is structurally incomplete.

○

Measure what nature contributes Ready to act

Colombia's national statistics agency (DANE) already has a decade of work on ecosystem accounts. A pilot in the Sinú Basin proved the methodology works. Colombia's national adaptation monitoring system (SIIVRA) will be fully operational by 2028. The CICC's climate finance committee (CGF) — which includes both DANE and the Ministry of Finance as formal members — can issue a policy guideline requiring ecosystem values to be included in public investment appraisal from 2028. No new legislation required.

○

Standardise the discount rate for ecological assets Administrative decision

Currently, the same climate investment can pass or fail a cost-benefit test depending on which analyst runs the numbers, because there is no standardised discount rate for irreversible ecological losses. The OECD explicitly recommends Colombia adopt standardised reference values. The National Planning Department (DNP) can issue this as an administrative guideline — not a law. It would directly determine whether carbon pricing reform clears cost-benefit tests.

The bottom line: The tools, institutions and data to fix Colombia's carbon pricing framework are already in place. The question is not whether the economics justify action — the numbers support reform across every plausible scenario. The question is whether the political will exists to use the instruments that already exist.
